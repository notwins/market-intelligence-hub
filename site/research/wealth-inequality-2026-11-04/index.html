Own the Assets. Understand the Borrowing.

Wealth, portfolios and disciplined carry

Research note | 4 November 2026 | Core dataset: Q2 2026 U.S. Distributional Financial Accounts

Prepared for the Market Intelligence Hub. Independent educational research, not an official bank publication or a personalised recommendation.

The evidence supports a positive case for owning a portfolio and understanding secured financing, not a blanket instruction to borrow. Wealthy households own a disproportionate share of financial assets. Portfolio-backed credit is a well-established wealth-management tool. A carry trade can add value when its income and expected return adequately compensate for financing, market, currency and liquidity risks. These are three related propositions, but they are not interchangeable. [1, 4, 5, 6, 7]

Central message: Build assets that can compound and produce income. Understand the liquidity those assets may support. Use borrowing selectively, at a size that allows the portfolio to survive stress.

Five key takeaways

  1. Ownership is concentrated. The top 10% held 68.9% of U.S. household net worth in Q2 2026. See the wealth chart.
  2. Financial assets matter. The same group held 88.1% of the broad equity-and-fund category, not 88.1% of its own portfolio. Compare asset ownership.
  3. Borrowing is a choice. Family-office surveys establish use of secured credit, not a rule that every wealthy household should borrow. See the survey chart.
  4. Positive carry is not total profit. Asset-price losses, FX changes and fees can outweigh coupon income after interest. Follow the calculation.
  5. Portfolio first, financing second. Keep stressed collateral headroom and a credible repayment route. Use the decision framework.

1. The ownership evidence: financial wealth is highly concentrated

The wealthiest 10% held 68.9% of total U.S. household net worth in Q2 2026, while the bottom half held 2.3%. The top 1% alone held 32.5%, up from 31.1% one year earlier. The next 9% held 36.4%, unchanged at the displayed precision. The middle 40% saw its share fall from 30.1% to 28.8%. These are shares of net wealth, not income, spending or the number of people who invest. [1, PDF p. 5]

Calculation: Top 10% share = 32.5% + 36.4% = 68.9%. Top 1% annual change = 32.5% − 31.1% = 1.4 percentage points. The top 10% row overlaps with the first two rows and must not be added to them. [1; author arithmetic]

Even within the top 1%, ownership is concentrated. The top 0.1% held 15.0% of net worth, compared with 14.1% a year earlier. This is a subgroup of the top 1%, not an additional group. A falling share does not, by itself, mean falling dollar wealth: the denominator may be growing faster than the group's holdings. [1, PDF p. 5; interpretation]

Layman story: Imagine dividing all net household wealth into 100 units. The wealthiest tenth receives 68.9 units, the next four-tenths receives 28.8, and the bottom half shares 2.3. The lesson is not that everyone can become wealthy by copying one trade. It is that participation in asset ownership is economically important.

Takeaway: The portfolio discussion belongs at the centre of wealth planning, not at the edge of a deposit discussion.

2. What the wealthy own: shares, debt securities and liquidity

The top 10% held 88.1% of the DFA category “corporate equities and mutual fund shares” and 79.3% of the displayed debt-securities category. Their ownership of financial assets was more concentrated than their ownership of real estate. This supports the argument that substantial wealth is associated with a financial portfolio, rather than property or cash alone. It does not reveal the ideal allocation for an individual investor. [1, PDF pp. 1–3]

Calculation: 50.9% + 37.2% = 88.1%; 38.6% + 40.7% = 79.3%; 23.4% + 35.3% = 58.7%. These are ownership shares within each category, not portfolio allocation percentages. “The top 1% owns 50.9% of this category” does not mean “50.9% of the top 1%'s portfolio is invested in it.” [1; author arithmetic]

The equity label needs care. The Federal Reserve's 18 September 2026 announcement says hedge-fund and private-debt-fund holdings remain inside the DFA corporate-equities-and-mutual-funds category, although the aggregate household accounts now identify them separately. Corporate equities also cover publicly traded and privately held corporations. Pension entitlements appear separately. Consequently, 88.1% is not a clean measure of the top 10%'s ownership of all listed U.S. stocks, nor a measure including every household's indirect pension exposure. [3]

The bond evidence is narrower than the everyday word “bonds.” The supplied table displays 79.3% for the top 10%'s debt-securities share, but the corporate-and-foreign-bonds rows are missing, shown as dots. Those dots are not zeros. Do not claim this file independently proves the same percentage for every corporate bond or every bond held through funds. [1, PDF p. 2]

Layman story: One household owns its home and bank deposits. Another also owns interests in companies, lending instruments and funds. The second household has more routes through which business profits, interest payments and asset revaluations can affect its wealth. Those routes can generate gains or losses.

Takeaway: The defensible message is “own an appropriate mix of assets,” not “put everything into the asset class that rich people dominate.”

3. Debt is not one thing: distinguish what households own from what they owe

The top 10% held 64.8% of household assets but 26.7% of household liabilities. The bottom 50% held 5.0% of assets and owed 29.5% of liabilities. This does not calculate either group's loan-to-value ratio, because total assets and total liabilities have different dollar denominators. It does show why the type and size of a balance sheet matter more than the mere presence of debt. [1, PDF pp. 1, 4]

The table does not identify a clean “carry-trade borrowing” category. The top 1%'s 43.5% share of other loans and advances is not equivalent to a 43.5% share of Lombard loans. These broad balance-sheet rows do not identify each loan's collateral, funding currency, investment purpose, term or interest rate. They cannot establish how many households borrow to buy bonds. [1; scope limitation]

A particularly easy error is to confuse mortgage assets with mortgage liabilities. On page 3, the top 1%'s 49.4% share of mortgages appears under loans owned as assets. On page 4, its share of home mortgages owed is 4.0%. One is a claim on a borrower; the other is a debt obligation. Reversing them would reverse the economic story. [1, PDF pp. 3–4]

Layman story: Owning a mortgage claim resembles being the lender collecting payments; owing a mortgage means being the borrower making them. Both use the word “mortgage,” but they sit on opposite sides of the balance sheet.

Takeaway: Productive assets and manageable financing can coexist. The FRED data does not support the claim that maximum borrowing is how the wealthy became wealthy.

Takeaway: Your strongest supported statement is that secured borrowing is an established part of sophisticated wealth management, used selectively rather than universally.

5. Why a portfolio matters even before leverage

A portfolio can serve several distinct functions: participation in growth, contractual or discretionary cash income, diversification and potential collateral. These functions should be designed together, but not confused. Shares can participate in business profits; bonds provide contractual claims subject to their terms and issuer performance; selected investments may be accepted as collateral. Neither distributions nor collateral eligibility guarantee a positive total return. [6, 7, 20, 21]

Wide table: scroll sideways to compare all columns.

Portfolio function Potential benefit Essential distinction
Ownership Exposure to profits and long-term capital growth Market prices and business outcomes remain uncertain
Income Coupons, dividends or distributions Cash distributions alone do not measure total return; changes in NAV and costs also matter
Diversification Less dependence on one business, property or market Correlations can rise during stress
Collateral Access to financing without an immediate asset sale Lending value is below market value and can change
Optionality More choices about spending, rebalancing and timing A credit line is not equivalent to unencumbered cash

Research supports the importance of portfolio composition, but not a simple causal promise. Richmond Fed analysis describes greater stock and business-equity exposure among wealthier households, compared with the central role of housing further down the distribution. Dallas Fed research links pandemic-period wealth-share movements to differing portfolios and asset prices. These findings support understanding the assets one owns; they do not show that buying shares or borrowing automatically produces wealth. [8, 10]

Wealth accumulation also reflects earnings, business creation, inheritance and unequal opportunities. A Cleveland Fed working paper identifies large earnings differences as a primary driver in its model, with bequests and return differences also important at the top. An IMF paper using Norwegian administrative data finds persistent return differences associated with wealth, risk, scale and other characteristics. These are not estimates of the return a new leveraged investor will earn. [11, 12]

Layman story: A salary is money earned through current work. A financial portfolio is a set of claims on assets and cash flows that can continue beyond that work. The aim is to build a second source of financial capacity, not to replace one uncertainty with an oversized bet.

Takeaway: The case for owning assets is stronger and more general than the case for borrowing to own more of them.

6. Secured lending, investment leverage and FX carry are different decisions

Secured lending describes the collateral; carry describes the economics of holding a position. A wealthy client borrowing against shares to meet a temporary liquidity need is not necessarily executing a carry trade. A loan used to buy additional assets creates investment leverage. Borrowing in one currency to hold assets in another adds a separate currency position. [6, 7, 13, 14]

Wide table: scroll sideways to compare all columns.

Structure What it does Main issue to examine
Unleveraged portfolio Buys investments with own capital Asset selection, valuation and risk capacity
Portfolio-backed liquidity loan Raises cash while retaining pledged investments Repayment source, collateral stability and permitted use
Same-currency investment financing Borrows to buy additional assets in the funding currency Asset return versus all-in loan cost, duration and margin risk
Cross-currency carry Funds assets using a different currency All of the above plus FX changes and hedge costs

Avoiding a sale can be economically useful when there is a credible repayment plan. A loan may bridge a timing mismatch, preserve an investment position or allow a different asset to be bought without first liquidating existing holdings. However, borrowing to finance ongoing consumption creates an interest obligation without necessarily adding an earning asset. Preserving a portfolio is not, by itself, proof that the loan is worthwhile. [6, 13; interpretation]

Facility rules are jurisdiction- and contract-specific. U.S. securities-backed lines of credit described by the SEC and FINRA are non-purpose loans that cannot be used to purchase or trade securities. By contrast, OCBC's Singapore wealth-financing page and UBS Singapore's Lombard guidance expressly describe investment uses. Do not transfer the rules of one product to another. [6, 7, 13]

Layman story: Borrowing against a shop to cover a short payment gap is different from borrowing to buy a second shop. Funding either loan in a currency the shop does not earn creates another decision. The same collateral does not make all three plans equally sensible.

Takeaway: Start with “What job must this loan perform?” before asking “What is the cheapest borrowing currency?”

7. The carry arithmetic: cash income is not total return

Positive cash carry means income exceeds financing costs over the measured period, not that the overall investment has made money. A bond can pay its coupon while its market price declines. A fund can make distributions while its net asset value falls. A discounted bond's yield to maturity is not the same as the coupon cash available to pay a loan today. [21; financial identities]

General identities, not a client simulation

For unchanged principal in one currency, define own capital E, loan principal L, gross assets A = E + L, asset total return r_A, borrowing return-cost r_L and fees F, all over the same period. There are no intermediate external capital flows in this identity.

Economic profit = A × r_A − L × r_L − F.

Return on own capital = r_A + (L/E) × (r_A − r_L) − F/E.

This algebra shows that leverage amplifies the difference between the asset's realised return and the financing cost. If that difference is negative, borrowing worsens the result. The identity does not supply a forecast for r_A.

Cash income after financing = cash received − interest paid − relevant cash fees. Principal repayment is a cash outflow that reduces debt; it is not an additional investment loss. Loan proceeds are not income. Asset appreciation is not cash until monetised. Accrued costs must still be recognised when measuring economic performance.

Layman story: A shop earns rent, and its owner pays loan interest. The difference can be positive even if the shop's resale value falls. An investor must monitor both the rental-style income ledger and the balance-sheet ledger.

Takeaway: A carry strategy needs positive economics and the ability to stay funded. Income alone cannot answer both questions.

8. Margin of safety: borrowing capacity is not a borrowing target

A collateral-backed loan is safer for the lender than an otherwise comparable unsecured exposure; it is not a guarantee of safety for the borrower. The lender's right to demand repayment or sell collateral can conflict with the investor's intended holding period. A sound asset held with fragile funding can still become a bad realised outcome. [6, 13, 22]

The collateral ledger

In a simplified single-currency structure, let a be the permitted advance ratio and V the collateral's market value.

Lending value = a × V. Actual LTV = L/V. Headroom = a × V − L.

Advance ratio, also called an allowed financing or lending ratio in some products, is a limit. Actual LTV is the borrowing already used relative to current collateral value. A haircut h corresponds to a = 1 − h in this simplified framework. Product names and calculation rules differ by lender.

For a portfolio, calculate each asset separately: lending value = sum of a_i × V_i. Compare this with all relevant credit usage, including any contract-specific liability adjustments, accrued interest, derivative obligations and currency conversion. One high-quality bond does not make every pledged position equally financeable.

The OCBC maximum-capacity illustration has no initial headroom under this simplified test: 70% × $2,500,000 − $1,750,000 = $0. A decline in collateral value or a cut in advance ratio creates a shortfall unless cash or additional collateral is available. This is why the maximum is not the same as a prudent target. [7; author arithmetic]

Independent liquidity is more reliable than unused capacity against the same falling assets. Cash or other readily available resources outside the stressed collateral pool can meet calls without simultaneously reducing the pool's lending value. An undrawn, uncommitted line may be reduced when it is most needed. [6, 13; interpretation]

Layman story: A bridge's maximum load is not an invitation to park at its limit during a storm. The purpose of unused capacity is to absorb movement, not to make a spreadsheet look inefficient.

Takeaway: Confidence should come from quantified headroom and repayment capacity, not from the word “secured.”

9. CHF, USD and carry: keep the currency risks separate

Borrowing cheaply in CHF to hold USD assets is both an investment decision and a currency decision. A USD asset can perform as expected while a stronger CHF increases the USD cost of repaying the loan. A Singapore investor then has a further reporting question: what is the resulting wealth worth in SGD? The currencies must be tracked separately rather than buried in one “net yield.” [14, 15, 22]

For a one-period position with unchanged CHF principal, let A_0 and A_1 be USD asset values excluding distributed cash, C be USD cash distributions, L_CHF be loan principal, and q be USD per CHF. Convert interest at the exchange rate applicable when paid.

USD profit = (A_1 − A_0) + C − L_CHF × (q_1 − q_0) − USD interest cost − fees.

If CHF strengthens against USD, q_1 is higher than q_0 and the loan-principal translation term reduces profit. The formula deliberately does not subtract the full loan principal as a new expense: principal already exists on the opening balance sheet. It assumes no changes in loan principal or other external capital flows.

A full FX hedge is not a way to retain the entire quoted interest differential for free. Forward exchange rates reflect interest-rate differences; cross-currency basis, bid-offer costs, tenor and bank spreads affect the actual hedged funding cost. The relevant comparison is the quoted all-in hedged cost for the matching period, not the CHF interest rate in isolation. [15]

History makes this distinction concrete. On 15 January 2015 the SNB ended its minimum exchange rate of CHF 1.20 per euro. In August 2024, BIS analysis documented how unwinding leveraged equity and FX positions, including yen-funded carry, amplified a market shock. Neither event proves that every carry position is inappropriate. Both show why funding-currency appreciation and collateral stress must be tested together. [14, 16]

Layman story: Receiving income in one currency while owing another is like running a shop whose supplier can reprice the debt in a different unit. The shop can trade well and still face a larger repayment bill.

Takeaway: A lower nominal loan rate is a starting input, not the final measure of cheaper financing.

Section sources: [14, 15, 16, 22]; the profit identity is an author derivation.

10. The Singapore application: portfolio first, financing second

Portfolio-backed financing is an established Singapore wealth-management service. OCBC lists bonds, shares, unit trusts and other approved assets among eligible collateral, describes interest-only tenors of up to six months with principal auto-rollover, and lists multiple drawdown currencies. Those public features are not a guarantee of renewal, a client-specific advance ratio or a current financing quote. [7]

The sensible sequence is to choose a portfolio that works without leverage, then test whether financing improves the plan. Start with liabilities, liquidity needs and investment horizon. Next select the asset mix and investment amounts. Only then compare funding currencies, total borrowing costs, collateral terms and stress outcomes. This is an analytical framework, not a fixed recommended allocation. [6, 22; interpretation]

The U.S. tax story should not be imported unchanged. IRAS says gains from selling shares and financial instruments held as personal investments are generally not taxable in Singapore. Trading or business circumstances and other tax jurisdictions may change the result. A Singapore client therefore should not assume that U.S. “borrow rather than sell” tax arguments create the same benefit for them. [17]

Layman story: Owning a home does not require borrowing against every available dollar of equity. A securities portfolio deserves the same discipline: useful assets first, suitable borrowing second.

Takeaway: Do not be afraid of a portfolio merely because prices fluctuate. Do not add leverage merely because the bank offers it.

11. The macro implication: asset prices and financing can reinforce each other

The distribution helps explain why strong markets and household financial pressure can coexist. Ownership of financial assets and consumer debt are distributed very differently. Rising equity valuations therefore need not improve every household's finances equally. Equally, the table does not provide a direct estimate of consumption growth or the spending response of each group. [1, 10]

A potential feedback loop is visible when asset ownership is combined with collateralised lending. Higher market values can raise lending capacity, which may enable further investment. In reverse, falling values and tighter advance ratios can reduce that capacity together. This is an analytical mechanism, not a claim that the FRED table measures how much current market activity is financed this way. BIS research documents the amplifying role of leverage and margin adjustments in market stress. [14; interpretation]

For an investor, this changes the monitoring dashboard. A market view should sit beside a funding view: asset valuations, issuer quality and concentration on one side; actual borrowing, loan resets, currency mismatches and available liquidity on the other. An attractive long-run investment thesis does not remove the need for short-run financing resilience. [6, 14, 22; interpretation]

Layman story: When the value of a building rises, its owner may be able to refinance. When values fall across a whole district and lenders become cautious, refinancing can become harder precisely when cash is needed. Securities-backed lending can follow the same direction of pressure, often at a faster pace.

Takeaway: The portfolio should be built to benefit from ownership without relying on permanently rising collateral values.

Section sources: [1, 6, 10, 14, 22]

12. Research library: what the papers actually contribute

The strongest research case combines measurement, portfolio behaviour and financing risk rather than looking for one paper that endorses leverage. The following primary-source reading list separates research findings from bank surveys and product descriptions. Working papers express their authors' analysis; they are not central-bank investment recommendations.

Takeaway: Evidence supports understanding and disciplined use of leverage. It does not turn leverage into a prerequisite for wealth.

13. Related reporting: the market is expanding, but headlines need denominators

News reports are useful context, not a substitute for the ownership data or loan contract. The following items are dated explicitly; older reporting is included for background rather than presented as this week's news.

27 October 2026 SGT: securities-backed lending reaches a wider client base

The Wall Street Journal reported a Deloitte estimate of $5.4 trillion in securities-based credit extended, versus roughly $1.6 trillion actually borrowed. The distinction matters: credit availability is not drawn debt. These are third-party estimates reported by the Journal, not a verified count of wealthy households using carry. [23]

30 July 2026: borrowing instead of selling investments

Barron's described wealthy clients using appreciated assets to fund liquidity needs without selling. The relevance is retained ownership and flexible financing, not proof that borrowing is always cheaper after tax or suitable for a Singapore investor. [24]

10 September 2025: wealth lending becomes a strategic banking priority

Reuters reported that Merrill was creating a team to expand credit for wealthy clients. Reported purposes included liquidity, taxes, purchases and maintaining investments. The variety of uses is precisely why wealth lending should not be equated with currency carry. [25]

Interpretation: The coverage supports the proposition that wealth financing is mainstream and commercially important. It also highlights a bank incentive: lending earns revenue while allowing client assets to remain invested. That makes a separate client-benefit test important, rather than treating availability as endorsement. [23, 25]

Takeaway: “Banks do a lot of this” is evidence of an established product, not proof that every offered loan is a good investment decision.

Section sources: [23, 24, 25]

14. Practical framework: turn fear into a financing plan

The objective is not to eliminate risk, but to select risks that the household can understand and carry through adverse conditions. A conservative unleveraged portfolio can be appropriate. A modest secured facility can also be appropriate. The decision depends on the client's overall finances, purpose, currency exposure and ability to meet obligations, not merely their wealth percentile. [6, 13, 22; analytical framework]

A useful portfolio-financing dashboard needs four separate views. First, asset value and investment performance; second, cash income and cash expenses; third, loan principal and FX translation; fourth, lending value and margin headroom. Keeping them separate prevents coupons being mistaken for profit, loan principal being subtracted twice, or a positive income spread disguising a collateral shortfall.

For client communication, lead with ownership and choice rather than prestige. “Wealthy people use this” can explain why a tool deserves consideration. It is not a suitability test. The more persuasive explanation is what the tool accomplishes for this client and how the plan survives a bad outcome.

Final layman story

A family has spent years building a set of income-producing assets. When a temporary opportunity or expense appears, it need not automatically sell those assets. A well-structured loan may preserve ownership and create flexibility. But the family still needs enough cash, collateral headroom and time to repay without being forced to sell during a downturn.

Another family can build the same habit of asset ownership without borrowing at all. Both approaches can be sensible. What matters is that the financing serves the portfolio and the household, rather than forcing both to serve the loan.

Final conclusion: Do not be afraid of owning a portfolio. Understand secured credit as a legitimate tool. Use carry only when the economics and the capacity to withstand losses make sense. Ownership is the foundation; leverage is optional.

15. Data audit and limitations

The DFA is an estimated distributional account, not a quarterly census of every household's brokerage account. It combines the Financial Accounts with the Survey of Consumer Finances, reconciles their concepts, interpolates between survey dates and estimates periods after the latest survey. Figures can be revised. The current methodology and classification notes should accompany any repeated chart. [2, 3, 18]

This report keeps the uploaded Q2 2026 snapshot as its numerical anchor. Dollar totals from earlier conversation text are not reproduced because they are not contained in the supplied share table and have not been independently reconciled for this report. No same-vintage historical series back to 1989 was reconstructed, and no claim that the observed year-on-year change is unprecedented is made.

Ownership concentration is not household behaviour or causation. A share table does not reveal the percentage of households with an account, how assets were acquired, which loans funded them, or whether borrowing generated their wealth. Households can also move between wealth groups. Rich people losing a wealth share and poorer people gaining one would not, by itself, establish better individual investment decisions.

Global surveys are not directly comparable with U.S. population accounts. The bank surveys use selected family-office respondents, different questions and different reporting conventions. Lending balances measure amounts, not borrower counts or motivations. Corporate-and-foreign-bond entries are missing in the supplied file; broad fund classifications and separately reported pensions limit claims about underlying securities. [1, 3, 4, 5]

No personalised trade is proposed. Actual decisions require current facility documentation, collateral eligibility, loan quotes, tax circumstances and full household cash-flow information. This report contains no invented client financing terms, current market rates or probability of success.
