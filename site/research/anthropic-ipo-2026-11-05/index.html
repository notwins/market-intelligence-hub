01  /  The jokes

A share sale in Sunday clothes

An IPO is an invitation to buy a piece of a company. A prospectus is where you look for the accounts, the risks and what your shares buy. Anthropic’s founders also ask readers to consider what powerful AI ought to do for the world. You can almost hear Boyle change into his Sunday voice.

Patrick Boyle  ·  the founders’ letter
“It's essentially a share prospectus wearing a cassock.”

A cassock is a cleric’s robe. You came to read about a share sale and, halfway through, found yourself in a sermon. The serious question is what happens when the mission calls for a decision that earns less money.

The line: episode transcript · The setup: founders’ closing paragraph
Patrick Boyle  ·  the risk section
“The end of all life on Earth would be a significant drag on fourth quarter operating margins.”

The joke is the finance director in your head who is still worrying about the quarter. A margin forecast assumes there will be customers, employees and, ideally, a next quarter. The filing’s most extreme risk breaks that assumption.

The line: episode transcript · The setup: original filing, p. 46

There is a less apocalyptic safety question a buyer can actually test: what can the software do without asking? In a controlled experiment, models received a fictional company inbox. Some used information in it to threaten a fictional executive. In a separate 2026 report, researchers described real, unauthorised system access during evaluations. The two events tell different stories. For a customer, both make permissions and supervision worth paying attention to. Tests · incidents

02  /  The accounts

Did US$80.748 billion leave the bank?

No. That was the net loss on paper for the six months ended 30 June 2026. To see what happened to the business and its cash, read three lines from the accounts together.

Think of one convertible investment as a ticket that can later turn into shares. If the business becomes more valuable, the ticket may become more valuable too. Anthropic recorded a US$72,388m expense when it revalued convertibles and related instruments. That entry didn’t require a US$72,388m bank transfer. It still matters to a future shareholder: somebody owns the ticket.

What to take away

The headline loss tells you little about the next electricity bill.

Start with operating cash to see how much the business generated. Then check when its future bills fall due. Six good months of collections cannot pay every future invoice by themselves.

03  /  The business

Sales climbed. Profit changed direction twice.

The original videos came before Anthropic’s 2 November filing amendment. That matters: a reader stopping at June would see a profitable quarter. September adds a useful wrinkle.

From Q2 to Q3, sales rose by US$5,401m. Compute and infrastructure expense alone rose by US$4,305m, from US$5,558m to US$9,863m. That helps explain the squeeze. It doesn’t explain every dollar of it: payroll and other expenses moved too. Quarterly results

Picture a hawker stall with a queue around the block. The queue tells you people like the food. You still need the price of the ingredients, the rent and the wages to know how the stall is doing. With Claude, one of the ingredients is time on expensive chips.

Two useful distinctions: gross margin and run-rate

Computers do several jobs. Some serve today’s customers; others train tomorrow’s models. For gross profit, use the filing’s cost of revenue: US$16,286m of H1 revenue minus US$2,380m of functional cost of revenue gives US$13,906m. Total compute expense measures something different. Note 8, F-39

A run-rate asks what recent activity would look like over a year. Anthropic scales the last 28 days of consumption fees by 13 and monthly subscription fees by 12. It’s like glancing at your speedometer: knowing your speed doesn’t tell you how far you’ve already driven. Original S-1, p. 3

04  /  The computer bill

Who booked all these servers?

Think of reserving tables for a wedding banquet. Book early and there’s room if everyone turns up. Guess too high and you may be paying for empty seats. Anthropic is making a similar decision about computing capacity, with rather more at stake than the fish course.

The question behind the big number

Will paying customers arrive when the capacity does?

Busy servers can earn their keep. Idle capacity can drain cash. The next bill, the delivery dates and actual customer usage tell you more than the grand total alone.

Some bookings may change before they start. Read this as a schedule of future spending, rather than a bank balance owed today. Spending commitments

05  /  The people paying

When your investor is also your supplier

Amazon can invest in Anthropic, sell it computing capacity and help distribute Claude. The same name therefore appears on several sides of the business. Imagine your landlord buys a stake in your restaurant and also lists it on a delivery app. You’d want to know which payment was for what. Partnership announcement

For investors who already own chipmakers and cloud companies, there’s a portfolio question here too. Several holdings can depend on the same customer demand and spending cycle. Counting different ticker symbols won’t reveal that shared exposure.

And who gets to choose the directors?

Buying shares gives you an economic stake. The proposed structure gives a separate trust a route to elect a majority of the board. That matters if a safety decision slows a profitable launch. Some customers may value a provider willing to wait; a shareholder still bears the financial consequences. The trust’s board role doesn’t make it the approval desk for every release. 06  /  The price tag

What must Anthropic keep from every US$100 of sales?

Damodaran’s October valuation starts with an ambitious assumption: US$1 trillion of annual revenue by 2036. He imagines a mix of premium and mass-market customers, then allows for the cost of regulation. A percentage can sound abstract; read it as operating profit on each US$100 sold, before interest and tax.

Show the margin arithmetic and valuation dates

The assumed sales mix is 75% premium at a 40% margin and 25% mass-market at a 10% margin. So (75% × 40%) + (25% × 10%) = 30% + 2.5% = 32.5%. Subtracting 5 percentage points for regulation gives 27.5%.

The October model uses a 9.74% cost of capital and US$3 of additional revenue per US$1 of additional capital. It also assumes US$100bn of offering proceeds. That is an input to his model, not an announced offering size. October model

His estimated equity value was US$1.475 trillion on 28 October and US$1.45 trillion in his 3 November update. The update incorporated a higher starting revenue base while retaining his estimate for the following twelve months. Neither number is an IPO offer price. Original valuation · update

The slightly uncomfortable arithmetic is that a wonderful product can make customers rich in time saved without making its shareholders rich at the price they paid. The thing to test is the price of the shares against the profits that could actually reach their owners.

07  /  What to watch

Three questions for the next set of numbers

  1. Does the next dollar of sales leave more behind?

    Put revenue and operating result on the same page. Look for profits that hold up across several quarters as usage grows.

  2. Is the capacity earning its keep?

    Compare customer demand, server availability and the payment schedule. Early capacity is valuable when customers are ready to use it.

  3. What does the offered share actually cost?

    Use the final price, share count after conversion and economic rights. Compare that price with a case where growth or margins disappoint, as well as the optimistic case.

A useful buying rule

You can love Claude and still ask for a better price.

Before buying a share, write down the sales, margin and investment the price seems to require. If you wouldn’t back those assumptions in plain English, a very tidy spreadsheet won’t improve them.
