Diversification.
Made simple.

Give your money more than one job. Build a portfolio that depends on several sources of return, so one disappointment has less power over the whole.

01 / The one-minute idea

Prepare for more than one kind of weather.

Think sunglasses and umbrellas.

A shop selling only sunglasses depends heavily on sunshine. Adding umbrellas gives it another source of sales when the weather changes.

Your portfolio can follow the same principle. Own investments with different underlying drivers, so you depend less on getting one forecast right.

The analogy explains dependence, not a perfect hedge. A quiet shopping day can hurt both businesses.

03 / A common trap

Three products can still contain the same bet.

Count the underlying exposures, not just the statements. A technology share, a technology fund and a broad equity fund may all expose you to the same companies.

Look inside each fund before adding more. The names on the packaging can differ while the drivers of gains and losses overlap.

FINRA, “Concentrate on Concentration Risk”, 15 Jun 2022. [4]

04 / Put the idea to work

Decide what each part is there to do.

JOB 01

Long-term growth

Broad equity exposure participates in business growth. Spread it across companies, industries and markets.

Risk to remember: share prices can suffer large falls, and markets can fall together.

JOB 02

Planned income

Suitable bonds can contribute contractual cash flows. Match credit quality, maturity and currency to the purpose.

Risk to remember: issuers can default; prices can fall when yields rise. Bond funds carry credit and interest-rate risk too.

JOB 03

Near-term needs

Accessible cash helps you meet upcoming spending. Match it to the currency and timing of those commitments.

Risk to remember: inflation erodes purchasing power; deposit terms and access conditions matter.

The right proportions depend on the person. Time horizon, ability to absorb losses and cash needs come before a fixed allocation. Adding a highly risky asset can increase the risk of an otherwise cautious portfolio.

06 / Say it simply

A client explanation you can use.

Let’s give your money different jobs. Some can work towards long-term growth, some can provide income, and some can stay accessible for your spending needs.

The aim is to reduce how much your whole portfolio depends on one investment doing well. We should check that the investments really bring different exposures.

Diversification can help make the journey easier to stay with. It still allows losses, and it will not beat the best-performing investment every time.

The workings, when you want them.

1. Exactly how the example is calculated

Both components finish at 108 from 100. A earns +20% then −10%; B earns −10% then +20%. The two periods are fictional and have no stated calendar length.

A: 100 × 1.20 × 0.90 = 108
B: 100 × 0.90 × 1.20 = 108
Mix after period 1: 50 × 1.20 + 50 × 0.90 = 60 + 45 = 105
Mix after period 2: 60 × 0.90 + 45 × 1.20 = 54 + 54 = 108

The mix is only 50/50 at the start. After the first period it holds 60 in A and 45 in B. We leave those holdings alone. Its second-period return is exactly (108 ÷ 105 − 1) × 100% = 20/7%, not 5%.

What if we reset the mix?

Rebalancing changes the strategy and the result. Resetting the 105 portfolio to 52.5 in each asset produces 52.5 × 0.90 + 52.5 × 1.20 = 110.25. The extra 2.25 index points arise from the rule and this deliberately alternating path. They are not a guaranteed diversification bonus.

2. Why how investments move together matters

Correlation describes how returns move together. A value of +1 means perfect positive linear co-movement in the measured returns. Zero means no linear correlation; it does not guarantee independence. A negative value means a tendency to move in opposite directions, not a promise that every loss is offset.

Negative correlation is not required for diversification to help. With ordinary positive portfolio weights, imperfect correlation can reduce portfolio volatility relative to the weighted average of the component volatilities. That does not guarantee less risk than the safest component on its own.

σ²portfolio = w²Aσ²A + w²Bσ²B + 2wAwBρσAσB

Here w means portfolio weight, σ means volatility (standard deviation of returns), and ρ means correlation. Volatility measures fluctuations around the average return; it is not a maximum-loss estimate.

3. Sharpe in plain English, then the correct formula

The Sharpe ratio compares excess return with the fluctuations that came with it. “Excess” means above the chosen risk-free reference. It is a measure of return per unit of volatility, not an interest rate or a safety score.

xt = portfolio returnt − risk-free returnt

Historical Sharpe = mean(xt) ÷ standard deviation(xt)

Same reward, more fluctuationIn the annual model above:
(8% − 2%) ÷ 20% = 0.3
Same reward, less fluctuationIn the annual model above:
(8% − 2%) ÷ 10% = 0.6

A higher ratio can come from an improved combination. But reducing volatility does not guarantee a higher Sharpe if expected excess return also falls. An investment with a lower standalone ratio can still help the whole portfolio if it contributes sufficiently different exposure.

For a fair comparisonWhat to keep consistent
Return seriesTotal returns, including distributions; the same fee basis and time window.
CurrencyOne reporting currency and a suitable benchmark in that currency.
FrequencyMatched daily, monthly or annual observations. Do not mix units.
InterpretationSeparate historical results from expected future returns. Show how many observations were used.
Use arithmetic excess-return observations for the standard historical ratio. Compounded growth is a different measure. A bond’s coupon or yield to maturity is not a total-return history.

Sharpe leaves important questions unanswered. It treats upward and downward fluctuations alike, does not describe every severe-loss possibility, and does not by itself tell you how a holding fits your other assets and liabilities.

4. When the argument helps—and when it needs qualifying

The main counterargument is real.

A concentrated winner can outperform a diversified portfolio. Holding several exposures means some will lag. Diversification seeks a more manageable range of outcomes; it does not promise the highest possible return.

What can happenWhy it mattersWhat to check
Several assets fall togetherA shared inflation, interest-rate or liquidity shock can overwhelm differences.Stress the whole portfolio and keep cash needs separate.
A new holding raises riskA volatile addition can increase total risk even if its correlation is low.Compare the complete mix before and after, including position size.
Currency dominatesForeign assets can gain locally but lose value in your spending currency.Measure the result in the currency of the goal; review any hedge costs.
Liquidity disappearsA quoted value may not be available when you need to sell.Check dealing frequency, redemption terms and independent accessible cash.
Rebalancing costs too muchFees, spreads and applicable taxes can erode the benefit.Use a considered review rule and account for costs.

History shows that shares and government bonds can fall together. BIS authors documented the US stock–government-bond return correlation turning positive in mid-2021 as inflation became more influential. Their December 2023 analysis is historical evidence of a changing relationship, not a reading of today’s correlation. [6]

What would change the proposed mix?

Change the mix when its purpose or risk has changed. New spending needs, shorter time horizons, larger repeated exposures, changing asset relationships or higher implementation costs can all justify a review. A new holding should earn its place by improving the overall fit.

The case for diversification is strongest when one outcome dominates your finances. If the existing portfolio already spreads risk well, adding another product may accomplish little. Review exposures first, then decide whether any change is needed.
