Three scenarios to try

Compare the note with buying whole NVDA shares at the start and keeping the change in cash. Both use the same payment and assume a sale at the same knock-out or maturity close.

If you receive shares below strike

Your purchase price is the strike. After delivery you own NVIDIA shares. The price can recover or fall further. Decide how much stock you want to keep and what sale price you would accept.

Keep the shares

You retain the full effect of later price moves. A fall below the strike is a loss at today’s valuation, even if you continue to hold.

Write a covered call

A call premium can offset part of the loss. You keep the stock downside and may have to sell at the call strike if assigned, giving up a larger rebound on those shares.

Consider a decumulator

A separate sale note can sell share-equivalent quantities at an agreed price over time. You may sell below a rising market. Check funding, remaining exposure and issuer risk.

Capital is at risk. If the normal note runs to maturity, the investor purchases the full scheduled share quantity. A falling share price reduces its value, and worthless shares can mean losing the amount invested in them. Issuer default can cause a total loss. Early exit can return less than the capital committed.

Notes, assumptions & sources

Overview calculations and chart sources

Overview cards show whole shares and rounded dollars. The calculations use exact cents. Displayed rounded amounts can differ by a dollar when added.

Sale cash enters the ledger in the month you sell. The ledger values unsold shares at each month’s close and assumes the remaining shares sell at KO or maturity.

Chart overlay dates and sources
Indicative strikes and guarantee periods

Strike and KO are percentages of the trade-day price. An 88.01% strike buys each delivered share at an 11.99% discount to the initial NVDA price.

OCBC’s quoted guarantee days are shown above the strikes. JPM quotes guarantee periods in months. Its day-by-day examples use an estimated trading calendar.

In the 23 November archive, the nine-month SI used 21, 41 and 61 guaranteed trading days for 1M, 2M and 3M respectively.

How share sizing and daily knock-out work

Round the purchase strike to cents. Divide the target budget by the strike and the number of months, then round to the nearest whole share for each month. The monthly quantity × strike × tenor gives the share purchase commitment. Round that commitment up to the next whole dollar for the payment. This can put the payment a little above or below the target.

A normal accumulator buys the full scheduled quantity while active, including when NVDA trades above the purchase strike. The purchase price stays at the strike. The chart joins your month-end prices with evenly spaced daily closes and checks every trading day. A daily close at or above the knock-out level ends accumulation, subject to the minimum period.

For an early knock-out, the model counts all guaranteed shares and refunds unused cash. After the minimum, purchases through the trading day before knock-out count. Each month’s whole-share quantity accrues across its model trading days. At a partial month, cumulative entitlement is rounded down to whole shares. The default exit assumes the shares sell at the knock-out close or, if there is no knock-out, at the maturity close. Actual fixing schedules, share allocation and settlement dates follow the final terms.

Holdings, cash refunds and P&L

All amounts in USD. At exit, total P&L equals proceeds from all share sales plus cash refunded, less the actual payment. The refund returns unused capital and counts once.

Holdings value includes shares still held at the month-end price, sale proceeds and refunds received to date, and capital still in the note at face value. Cash and simulated prices display to the nearest dollar. P&L and percentage returns round down. Tap a figure for the underlying calculation. An early unwind can have a different value.

Probability cone

The shaded range is a model interval for the share price at each future month. It assumes independent normal log returns and a flat median price. It does not estimate the chance of a knock-out or a positive note return. Large gaps, volatility changes and unusual returns can put actual prices outside the cone.
