1. Separate facts, interpretation and scenarios

Each publication should distinguish reported facts from analysis and opinion, hypothetical calculations from actual market prices, and current data from historical observations. A model-derived price or forecast should never be styled as if it were an official issuer or exchange quote.

The central claim of a report should be understandable without the chart. Charts should show units, date or cutoff, source and enough context to avoid distorted comparisons.

2. Date and source discipline

Time-sensitive reporting should carry a Singapore-time publication timestamp and a clearly labelled market-data cutoff or overseas session date. A Friday Singapore morning report can be based on Thursday's US session; those are not the same date, and they should be described as such.

Priority should go to primary documents where appropriate: issuer filings, government and central-bank releases, official data, prospectuses and term sheets. Trusted professional reporting, including Reuters when used, should be properly attributed and linked. Secondary commentary should not be mistaken for a primary record.

Source links should support the specific figure or assertion next to which they appear. A general bibliography does not substitute for evidence on a contested point.

3. Research status and corrections

A publication may be labelled Verified, Analysis, Scenario, Preliminary or Correction only if its current review status justifies the label. “Verified” means the material point or number was checked against identified evidence; it does not mean every prediction is correct.

When a material error is found, update the page, describe the correction with a date, and avoid silently rewriting a historical thesis as if it were the original conclusion. If sources conflict, report the disagreement rather than inventing certainty.

4. Mathematical transparency

Calculators and charts should disclose significant assumptions, cashflow timing, units, formulas and data origin. Distinguish nominal from real returns, yield from coupon, realised from unrealised P&L, and assets from debt and capital contributions.

For financed portfolios, net asset value after any FX translation must not have that same adjustment subtracted again. Comparison calculations should not mix cashflows from different periods or claim precision beyond the source data.

5. AI assistance and human review

AI can assist with research collection, language, visualisation and code generation, but it can hallucinate prices, names, legislation and citations. Important prices, issuer terms, calculations, financial claims and quotations should be tested against their sources before publication. Machine-generated explanations should be marked or described where that context matters.

Automation schedules and scraped feeds do not guarantee completeness, continuity or timeliness. A missed update is not a signal that the market did not change.

6. Conflicts of interest and employment boundaries

Writers and contributors may have direct or indirect exposure to instruments discussed, including personal holdings, model positions or hedges. Material interests should be disclosed when relevant and legally permissible. A public article is not an official recommendation from a writer's employer or associated financial institution unless expressly authorised and identified as such.

The project should never publish client-confidential, employer-proprietary, inside or unpublished price-sensitive information. Institutional branding, research extracts and professional titles must not imply endorsement, licensing or regulatory review that has not occurred.

7. Copyright, quotations and third parties

Quote only what is lawfully permitted or licensed, make source attribution specific, and prefer original charts over copying protected charts. Linking to Reuters, bank research or Morningstar does not establish the right to republish their full articles, pricing feeds or images. Third-party logos and trademarks remain the property of their owners.

8. Corrections and transparency

Material factual errors should be corrected transparently, with the correction and its date recorded. Do not send confidential account details in a public issue. Once operational, an article should show its original date and material revision history, not merely a “live” label.

10. Text and tagging

Match the meaning before applying the colour. A theme does not replace the fixed company or keyword identity. Keep a visible name or ticker beside the colour, and preserve the approved light-mode and dark-mode text/background pair.

  1. Validate the entity and context, then match the longest valid whole phrase. Numeric priority breaks equal-span matches only after identity is valid.
  2. Use whole-word boundaries: US must not match USD; Fed must not match FedEx. Preserve punctuation in OPEC+, S$NEER and qualified tickers.
  3. Ordinary phrases may be case-folded. Short tickers and ambiguous acronyms need the correct case and financial context. Leave unresolved BOC, C, SE, AI or DE unmapped.
  4. Keep USD/SGD and SGD/USD distinct. A company-name mention alone does not establish a listing, share class, headquarters or index membership.
  5. Retain the original V1 mappings, then the specific reconciled extensions, then the documented generic fallback. Never replace a company colour with a guessed brand colour.
  6. One story may carry several independent tags. A single text span has one dominant semantic colour. Keep entity, location, instrument, story status, evidence status and market direction separate.

11. Sections and separators

Make the reading order visible. Use numbered headings, generous space, a restrained 1px separator and short paragraphs. Keep body text around 16px with at least 1.5 line spacing; use roughly 24–32px section headings and adapt the layout instead of shrinking everything on a phone.

12. Formatting checklist

  • Required prompt reference: every article and posting prompt must link to this editorial policy and apply it alongside the current approved colour and tagging sources.
  • Start with the answer: a clear title, reporting cutoff and one to five supported takeaways.
  • Choose a theme deliberately: follow the question and the route table. Keep semantic identity colours separate.
  • Explain every useful visual: title, date or period, instrument, units, source, “How to read this”, finding and limitations. Preserve exact values where rounding matters.
  • Show honest comparisons: ordinary bars begin at zero; line scales are labelled; actuals and projections look different; missing data stays missing.
  • Check readability: exact tokens, contrast on the real background, visible labels, mobile layout, table scrolling, links and printed output.
  • Keep the review honest: automated checks validate declared tokens and required structure. They do not prove source accuracy, suitable tagging, legal clearance or rendered accessibility.

New authored research HTML follows the existing publication gate. Older reports are preserved; generated watchlist reports retain their separate checks and are not certified as V3. This addition documents the design standard and does not turn the draft legal policy into a final approved notice.

Reference style

Use my DBS and STI banks report as the main reference for its card layout, charts, explanations and overall presentation. Inspect it first. Adapt the design to the new topic rather than copying its figures or conclusions.

My colour and presentation standard

Apply my current approved Colour & Tagging Standard. The Excel governs keywords, tickers, aliases, tags and colour mappings. The matching PDF governs visual design, charts and layout. Follow the documented precedence rules and do not invent or overwrite mappings.

Each report can have a different colour theme and background shades, while keeping approved colours for the same companies, terms and concepts consistent. If the current standard cannot be accessed, say so and continue the research without claiming it has been applied.

Research and writing

Start with one to five key takeaways. Explain what happened, why it matters, what the evidence supports, the outlook, risks and what could change the conclusion.

Use plain English, short paragraphs and point form where helpful. Include a bold key takeaway in each paragraph, but avoid making everything bold. Explain unfamiliar terms and make the report easy to understand without sounding condescending.

Separate verified facts, source opinions, forecasts and your own analysis. Include counterarguments and material risks. Cite sources and distinguish the date of the data from the date it was published. Don’t invent figures or present forecasts as guarantees.

Charts and market context

Inspect the charts and data already available on my Market Intelligence Hub and reuse suitable components and verified data where practical. Where relevant to the research, include:

  • Prices of the security, commodity or index discussed.
  • Bond yields, yield curves or credit spreads.
  • Relevant FX pairs.
  • S&P 500 / SPX prices or performance.

Include only charts that help explain the report. Identify the instrument, currency, units, source, data date and whether prices are adjusted or include dividends. Label FX direction and yield tenor clearly. Do not describe dated data as live.

Targets, scenarios and calls

Where the research provides them, add base-case, bull-case or bear-case levels, targets and calls to the relevant chart. Label each with its source, date, horizon, assumptions and units. Clearly distinguish actual data from forecasts.

If only a target level is available, show a labelled target line or marker. Don’t invent a forecast path, missing target, probability or analyst call. Explain qualitative scenarios in a card rather than assigning them invented numbers. Show the reference price and date for any calculated upside or downside.

Card style and visual explanations

Aim for roughly half meaningful charts, tables and diagrams and half concise explanation. Each substantive card should have:

  • A clear takeaway title.
  • A relevant chart, table, diagram or comparison.
  • A short layman explanation of how to read it and why it matters.
  • Source, date and units where applicable.

Choose the visual that best explains the point; don’t add decorative charts just to fill space. Use direct labels and avoid misleading scales, tiny text and clipped headings. Ensure the report works on mobile and remains understandable in greyscale.

File size and checks

Keep files small for later upload to Market Intel. Use lightweight code-drawn visuals and editable charts rather than large embedded images. State the final file size. Check figures, calculations, chart labels, links and the rendered desktop and mobile layouts. Clearly identify any unresolved gaps.
