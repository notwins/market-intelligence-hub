01 / RESEARCH

IFI SPX Returns, 0%-9% Payoffs and Zero-Cost Collars

Recommendation: present the 0%-9% section as a historical scenario calculator, with a separate options-pricing feasibility panel. IFI is a research-module label here; its product terms and actual performance are not assumed.

The floor helped in 17 of 65 years; the cap constrained 38. For complete calendar years 1961-2025, 26.15% of price returns were negative, 15.38% were between 0% and 9% inclusive, and 58.46% exceeded 9%. These are observations from one historical sample, not forecast probabilities. [S1; own calculations]

Smoother annual outcomes came with lower full-period growth. Starting at 100, SPX price returns compounded to 11,780; the hypothetical annually reset transformation reached 3,872. Their CAGRs were 7.61% and 5.79%. A separately labelled dividend-reinvested comparator reached approximately 75,459. [S1-S2; own calculations]

A 9% cap and 0% floor do not establish zero-premium availability. The mathematical clipping rule assumes the desired payoff is provided each year. Only contemporaneous, executable option quotes can establish the net premium for a particular pair of strikes and expiry. No such historical quote backtest is claimed here.

Show the benefit and the sacrifice together. A paired display of losses avoided and upside surrendered is more informative than a floor-only story. The visible label should read: Hypothetical annual reset; price returns; before costs; not actual product performance.

Research date: 5 November 2026 (Singapore). Main dataset ends in 2025; all 2026 year-to-date observations are excluded. Display values are rounded; source precision and calculation outputs are preserved in the audit files.

02 / RESEARCH

Three evidence layers must remain separate

Historical observations, a payoff formula and an executable strategy answer different questions. The interface should make the evidence level visible before the user compares returns.

Layer 1 describes what the index did. The price series is observed market history as compiled by the stated source. An index itself is a reference measure; buying an ETF, a stock basket, futures or an insurance/structured contract introduces additional mechanics and costs.

Layer 2 asks a conditional mathematical question. Applying g(R) = min(max(R, 0), 0.09) to each complete one-year return says what the outcome would be if that transformation were available at every reset. It says nothing about option premiums, issuer solvency, asset-management fees or withdrawal rules.

Layer 3 requires a trading rule and contemporaneous prices. A historical collar study must specify its underlying, option contracts, available strikes, expiries, bid/ask treatment, settlement, funding, distributions and position sizes. Changing the strikes to obtain zero premium changes the floor/cap path.

No inspection of the existing IFI tool is implied. This report proposes a methodology and review plan. Its current code, return series, observation calendar, product documents and cashflow treatment would need to be inspected before implementing or validating a product-specific result.

Define the dataset before showing the result

The main view uses one consistent annual endpoint series for 1961-2025. The chosen 65-year window starts after the modern S&P 500 launch and avoids mixing predecessor history into the headline.

Price-return source. The NYU Stern/Damodaran HTML table dated January 2026 provides S&P 500 levels from year-end 1960 to year-end 2025. The starting level is 58.11 and the ending level is 6,845.50. Each return is the ratio of successive calendar-year-end levels minus one. Levels retain their published precision; annual percentage returns are recomputed, not copied from rounded display percentages. [S1]

Index definition and history. The headline refers to the S&P 500 USD price index, with no dividend reinvestment. S&P DJI dates the current index's launch to 4 March 1957; earlier series require a clearly marked predecessor/reconstructed-history treatment. This main sample contains no pre-launch years. It is not the longest possible historical sample. [S3-S4]

Comparator source. The total-return series uses Slickcharts' published dividend-reinvested annual figures over exactly the same 65 years. Its inputs are rounded to two decimal percentage points. The resulting long-run wealth is therefore an approximation to the provider's total-return history, not a reconstruction from full-precision daily SPTR levels. [S2; S5]

Source versions matter. The linked NYU XLS downloaded during this research was older than the current HTML table. The calculations therefore use the archived HTML-derived CSV, with source URLs, retrieval date and hashes. A reproducible refresh must check the actual last observation, not trust a file name or page title.

The date rule is deliberately simple. Use the final available trading close of each calendar year. Exclude incomplete years from annual counts, CAGR and frequency charts. A separately shown YTD card must say YTD, show its observation date, and never count as a completed one-year option period.

04 / RESEARCH

Return frequencies: keep the boundaries exact

The cap would have bound more than twice as often as the floor in this sample. That frequency does not establish whether protection was good value; the size of each affected return also matters.

Classify before rounding. The buckets are R < 0%; 0% <= R <= 9%; and R > 9%. Exact 0% and exact 9% belong to the middle bucket. Do not implement the middle bucket as a rounded-label comparison, and do not silently change the inequalities when filtering.

2011 is a useful audit test. The source levels fall from 1,257.64 to 1,257.60. The computed price return is approximately -0.003181%, so it belongs in the negative bucket although it can display as 0.00%. Moving it to the middle bucket would undercount losses by one. [S1; own calculation]

A histogram explains shape; a count table provides precision. Use uniform-width bins for the distribution and mark both thresholds. Keep the exact counts and denominator alongside it. The 9% line need not coincide with a histogram-bin edge.

05 / RESEARCH

Year-by-year clipping makes the exchange visible

The same rule replaces losses with zero and cuts every annual gain above 9%. Both effects must be visible in the main chart.

Use bars for the historical return and markers for the transformed outcome. This allows readers to see the removed loss in a bad year and the sacrificed return in a strong year without comparing two disconnected charts. Show the same annual dates and a shared vertical scale.

Small positive years pass through unchanged. For example, the source-level return for 2004 is approximately 8.99445%, so it remains below the cap. In 1965, the return is approximately 9.06195%, so it is clipped to 9%. Near-threshold values should be available in an exact-value tooltip. [S1; own calculations]

The floor is a maturity rule. A year containing a large market decline can still finish positive. The clipping function uses the beginning and ending observations only; it cannot demonstrate what an actual options position was worth at the intervening low.

The public audit notes explain verification and source access without republishing the raw annual series. No current-year partial period is included.

The hypothetical path finished with 67.13% less wealth than the price-index path over this full sample. Its smoother annual outcomes did not compensate for the repeated upside cap.

Multiply growth factors; do not add returns. Raw wealth is 100 times the product of (1 + R_t). Hypothetical wealth replaces R_t with g(R_t). Both paths use the same annual endpoints, start at 100, reinvest each hypothetical annual credit and have no external contributions or withdrawals.

07 / RESEARCH

Quantify both sides of the trade-off

The hypothetical floor removed large losses, but the cap surrendered gains more often. Frequency and magnitude should be shown together.

Avoided downside. In the 17 negative years, the average hypothetical uplift was 13.18 percentage points per affected year. The largest single-year improvement was 38.49 percentage points in 2008, when the source price return was -38.48579% and the clipped return was zero. [S1; own calculations]

Sacrificed upside. In the 38 years above 9%, the average removed gain was 11.14 percentage points per affected year. For 1995, the price return was approximately 34.11%, so approximately 25.11 percentage points were surrendered. The latest complete year, 2025, changed from approximately 16.39% to 9%. [S1; own calculations]

Arithmetic attribution is not cumulative investment performance. Summing the annual floor uplift gives 224.12 percentage points; summing the annual cap reduction gives 423.41 percentage points. These sums reconcile annual arithmetic effects only. They must not be presented as a cumulative return, money saved, insurance profit or terminal-wealth attribution.

The mean-return bridge and the CAGR comparison answer different questions. Average annual price return was 8.93%. The floor added 3.45 percentage points per calendar year on average, while the cap removed 6.51, leaving 5.87%. The CAGRs were 7.61% and 5.79%; compounding and return variability explain why these are not the same as the arithmetic means.

08 / RESEARCH

Zero sampled drawdown is not a daily guarantee

The hypothetical path has zero annual-endpoint drawdown by construction. This is an identity of the clipping formula, not evidence that an investable portfolio never falls in value.

Measurement frequency changes the question. The main drawdown uses only calendar-year endpoints, including the initial value of 100. SPX's largest observed decline on that grid was 41.92%, from the 1972 year-end high to 1974 year-end. The total-return comparator's 37.61% maximum occurred from 1999 year-end to 2002 year-end. [S1-S2; own calculations]

An option position has a price before it matures. Its value reflects the remaining time, volatility surface, rates, expected dividends and the current underlying level. An early sale can realise a loss even when the maturity payoff has a nominal floor. The put's maturity amount is not a constant daily cash value. [S9-S11]

Annual endpoints conceal both crashes and recoveries. A positive annual close does not mean the market stayed above its starting level all year. The module should label its drawdown frequency beside the metric and avoid drawing an invented smooth daily path between annual hypothetical credits.

No daily or monthly mark-to-market series for the hypothetical collar has been calculated. A daily collar drawdown requires daily portfolio and option valuations, with the relevant funding and collateral cashflows.

09 / RESEARCH

Regime examples should include awkward evidence

The transformation can help in a bear or poor-return period and lag sharply in a sustained bull market. Selected windows teach this dependency; they cannot establish a universal advantage.

A bear market highlights the maturity protection. Over 1973-1974, 100 fell to 58.08 in the price series while the hypothetical path stayed at 100. Over the 2008-2009 crisis and rebound, the corresponding values were 75.94 and 109.00. [S1; own calculations]

A strong bull market highlights the ceiling. During 1995-1999, price wealth rose to 319.91, whereas five successive 9% hypothetical credits produced 153.86. During 2010-2019, the outcomes were 289.73 and 182.80. [S1; own calculations]

A poor decade can still reward the annual floor in the hypothetical scenario. In 2000-2009, price wealth ended at 75.90 and the hypothetical path at 150.52. Call this a lost decade or a choppy decade with a negative price return, not a calm or flat market. It contained large losses and rebounds; the annual reset was central to the hypothetical result. For a nearly flat year-end example, use 2011: its price return was approximately -0.003181%, which the rule changes to zero. Neither example proves that the intra-year path was quiet.

Use broad subperiods as a check against cherry-picking. Price versus clipped CAGRs were 4.33% versus 5.30% in 1961-1980; 12.05% versus 6.08% in 1981-2000; and 6.80% versus 5.94% in 2001-2025. These remain historical scenarios, not executable collar returns.

12 / RESEARCH

At maturity, the collar has a simple payoff

For price exposure alone, a long underlying plus a 100-strike put minus a 109-strike call creates a 100-to-109 terminal value. Net premiums and other cashflows determine the investor's actual return.

Normalise the starting reference level to 100. Assume one unit of matching price exposure, a put strike Kp = 100, call strike Kc = 109, identical one-year maturities, matched notionals and settlement observations, and no costs. Set aside any cash dividends. The terminal price-related amount is V_T = S_T + max(100 - S_T, 0) - max(S_T - 109, 0).

The payoff identity is conditional on the premium assumption. If net option premium is zero, the initial price exposure costs 100 and (V_T / 100) - 1 equals g(R). If a debit is paid, the same terminal payoff does not produce a zero floor on total cash outlay. The numerical example is a payoff demonstration, not a traded quote. [S8; own derivation]

13 / RESEARCH

One maturity and repeated annual resets are different

An annually reset 0%-9% transformation is path-dependent at the reset dates. A single multi-year option payoff uses a different rule and must have a different display.

Reset the base consistently. At each annual boundary, the hypothetical strategy applies the next year's clipped index return to its own accumulated wealth. It does not continue to protect the original index level forever. A trading implementation would have to resize the underlying and options to that new wealth, allow for contract granularity, and pay the new market premium or choose new strikes.

A round trip demonstrates the distinction. Assume an invented two-year price path of +20% followed by -16 2/3%. The index returns to its starting value: 1.20 x (5/6) = 1.00. Annual clipping produces 1.09 x 1.00 = 1.09. Clipping the full two-year price return once produces 1.00. This is a mathematical example, not a historical case.

The cap's time basis must be explicit. A 9% ceiling per one-year segment is not the same as a 9% total ceiling over five years, nor a 9% monthly ceiling. A multi-year maturity also has different pricing, counterparty exposure and available contracts.

Monthly-start observations are not twelve independent portfolios unless the capital is allocated that way. Do not compound all overlapping twelve-month observations into one wealth series. To study a staggered arrangement, explicitly allocate capital across cohorts, identify funding dates, and aggregate their cashflows and marked values.

Product crediting can be a separate engine. If documentation specifies segment timing, participation rates, deductions, averaging, lock-ins or other rules, implement those exact terms separately. Nothing in this report establishes that the existing IFI module uses an annual-reset collar or any particular product mechanics.

14 / RESEARCH

Zero premium must be solved from market prices

You can fix a floor and solve the cap, or fix a cap and solve the floor. Fixing both at 0% and 9% leaves the net option premium as a market-determined result.

Use the executable sides of the market. For a long protective put and short call, a conservative quoted debit is put ask minus call bid, multiplied by matched quantities and contract multipliers. Midpoint equality is an indicative comparison, not evidence of a fill. A positive call-minus-put credit is not exact zero premium unless its handling is specified.

Zero premium is narrower than zero cost. Commissions, exchange and clearing fees, bid-ask spreads, slippage, funding and collateral costs remain. The short call also gives up economic upside. The underlying still requires capital or financing; a zero-premium options overlay is not a zero-capital investment. [S8-S10]

An illustrative model produces a debit at the requested strikes. With S0 = 100, Kp = 100, Kc = 109, T = 1, flat volatility 20%, a 3% continuously compounded risk-free rate and 1.5% continuous dividend yield, the model put costs 7.0771 and the call raises 5.0107: a 2.0664 debit per 100. [S11; own model calculation]

15 / RESEARCH

Pricing assumptions explain why the cap can change

The illustrative zero-premium cap is 3.30%, not 9%, under the base assumptions. Changing market inputs can materially change the available trade-off.

Base-case alternatives. Keeping the 100 put, the flat-volatility model solves a call strike of 103.3029. Keeping the 109 call instead, it solves a put strike of 95.4068, equivalent to a -4.5932% price floor. These are continuous-strike model outputs; neither is asserted to be listed or tradable.

Paying for protection changes the return denominator. If the model's 2.0664 debit is paid in addition to 100 for the exposure, the terminal range of 100 to 109 is approximately -2.02% to +6.79% on the 102.0664 outlay, before dividends, funding and other costs. A strike floor of 100 is not a zero-loss floor on that larger outlay.

Skew must be treated strike by strike. A higher implied volatility on the put relative to the call generally makes the desired protection harder to finance. At the fixed 100 and 109 strikes, changing only the assumed put volatility from 20% to 24% raises its model value to 8.6243 and the net debit to 3.6136. This is a two-leg sensitivity, not a calibrated arbitrage-free surface; no solved skewed cap is claimed.

Put-call parity provides a useful check. For European options with common strike K, continuous dividend yield q and rate r, C - P = S0 exp(-qT) - K exp(-rT). At K = S0 and r <= q, an out-of-the-money call cannot fund the at-the-money put in this idealised setting. The report does not extrapolate this condition into a claim about every historical market. 16 / RESEARCH

What determines whether the premiums match?

The floor, cap and zero-premium condition cannot all be treated as freely chosen constants. The option market prices the protection and the upside surrendered for a particular date and maturity.

Moneyness is only one input. A put struck at the current spot index level is spot-at-the-money; it need not be at the forward level. The forward incorporates financing and expected distributions. Quoting a 9% out-of-the-money call relative to spot should be explicit.

Listed contracts impose practical boundaries. Exactly 100% and 109% strikes relative to a changing index level may not exist on the listed strike grid. A calendar-year maturity may not match a suitable listed one-year expiry. Rounding strikes or changing dates changes the payoff and the premium; record these choices rather than hiding them. [S6-S7]

Use a whole quote curve when solving a strike. Interpolated model values can explain sensitivity, but a traded backtest should select available strikes under a declared rule. Midpoint interpolation and fractional contracts do not establish execution. The option quote timestamp, tenor and bid/ask basis belong next to every solved cap. [S9-S11]

17 / RESEARCH

Dividends and settlement prevent a casual comparison

SPX price return, total return and an investor's account return are different series. The module should name the series beside every chart.

A price index excludes dividends. A total-return index includes their reinvestment according to the index methodology. S&P DJI separately defines net-total-return calculations incorporating withholding adjustments. Do not take an annual dividend yield, append it casually to every clipped result and call the output an exact self-financing collar. Timing, reinvestment and hedge resizing matter. [S4-S5]

A stock basket or ETF can distribute cash. Direct ownership may receive dividends, subject to tax and expenses. The price component can be hedged, while the dividends remain a separate cashflow or are reinvested. Retained dividends can also take the total account outcome beyond the price-only ceiling; costs can reduce it below a price-only floor. Reinvestment can change exposure and notional matching. ETF expenses and tracking differences also separate its price from the reference index.

SPX options settle in cash and use European exercise. One full-size SPX contract has a USD 100 multiplier per index point. Traditional AM settlement uses component opening prices; SPXW PM settlement uses closing prices. An annual close-to-close scenario is not automatically identical to the settlement value of a particular contract. [S6-S7]

Do not assume a basket is physically deliverable against SPX. Gains or losses on cash-settled options become cash obligations; shares are not transferred to satisfy the SPX call. A portfolio hedge must match the economic notional and account for basis, tracking and settlement differences. A beta hedge may reduce systematic risk without creating an exact floor.

ETF/equity options introduce a different assignment mechanism. American-style short calls can be assigned before expiry, including around ex-dividend dates. That can alter holdings and cashflows. This early-assignment issue should not be attributed to European SPX options. [S6; S8]

18 / RESEARCH

Net outcomes depend on the investment wrapper

A maturity floor addresses a specified payoff, not every risk in the account. The report should show an explicit limitations panel beside the key figures.

Premiums, trading costs and financing. Buy-at-ask/sell-at-bid implementation, commissions, fees and slippage reduce returns. Financing the underlying or an option debit creates a separate cashflow. Funding rates can improve one part of an option quote while increasing the cost of a financed investment.

Collateral and liquidity. Broker and clearing requirements can change the cash or collateral needed to carry a position. Economic coverage by a stock basket does not guarantee identical regulatory margin treatment. A maturity hedge does not remove the need to meet interim obligations or the risk of selling assets at a poor time.

Taxes and distributions. Tax depends on the instrument, ownership structure and investor circumstances. Do not transplant US option-tax treatment into an unspecified Singapore investor account. The research figures are pre-tax; no tax rate or personal suitability assumption is applied.

Currency and purchasing power. These results measure nominal USD index exposure. For an SGD-based account, currency conversion is a separate source of gains or losses: (1 + USD return) x (ending USD/SGD / starting USD/SGD) - 1. A 0% USD price floor is not an SGD floor and does not protect purchasing power against inflation.

Issuer, early exit and actual product rules. A contractual product may expose holders to an issuer/guarantor or other counterparties and may apply early-exit valuation, surrender terms, deductions, participation limits or reset rights. Which of these apply must come from its documents. No such terms, issuer identity, guarantee or charges are assumed for IFI here.

The scope of protection needs a visible label. State: maturity only; specified reference exposure; gross or net basis; subject to the stated implementation and counterparty assumptions. Keep the language close to the headline instead of relying on a remote disclaimer.

This is research and presentation analysis. It does not recommend a product, transaction size or personal investment strategy.

The price-history window is longer than the instrument history. SPX options were introduced on 1 July 1983, so the pre-1983 years cannot be described as a listed-SPX-options track record. Contract conventions and available expiries also changed over time. [S14]

The required archive was not obtained. Closing index levels and a volatility index do not supply these missing trades.

A realistic rule must handle missing matches. Reusable formulas and calculation outline

The audit reproduces the historical and hypothetical headlines from frozen inputs. The reviewed calculation code uses stored source precision and separates calculation from display rounding.

Annual price return. R_y = P_y / P_(y-1) - 1. Hypothetical return: g_y = min(max(R_y, 0), 0.09). The classification is R_y < 0, 0 <= R_y <= 0.09, or R_y > 0.09.

Compounding and CAGR. W_0 = 100; W_y = W_(y-1) x (1 + R_y), with g_y substituted for the hypothetical series. Cumulative return = W_N / 100 - 1. CAGR = (W_N / 100)^(1/N) - 1.

Volatility and drawdown. Annual volatility is the sample standard deviation of the N annual returns, with denominator N - 1. No square-root-of-12 scaling is applied to annual returns. Drawdown at sampled date t = W_t / max(W_0,...,W_t) - 1; maximum drawdown is the minimum of those values.

Floor benefit and cap sacrifice. A_y = max(-R_y, 0); B_y = max(R_y - 0.09, 0). Then g_y = R_y + A_y - B_y. Annual sums of A and B are arithmetic attribution, not compounded returns.

Rolling one-year sensitivity. R_12,m = P_m / P_(m-12) - 1 on a verified complete monthly grid. If a month is missing, stop or explicitly mark the affected windows; do not silently forward-fill. Do not compound overlapping twelve-month outcomes into one investment path.

European model illustration. d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r - q + sigma^2/2)T] / (sigma sqrt(T)); d2 = d1 - sigma sqrt(T). C = S exp(-qT) N(d1) - K exp(-rT) N(d2); P = K exp(-rT) N(-d2) - S exp(-qT) N(-d1). N is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. The model is used only to explain pricing sensitivity.

Reproduce in order. Validate dates and uniqueness; calculate unrounded returns; apply buckets and clipping; compound identical dates; calculate metrics; reconcile terminal price wealth to 100 x final/initial level; export the annual audit; then round the displayed labels. The reviewed Python code and frozen source snapshots implement this sequence; raw snapshots are not republished here.
