MAS and SNB: policy, inflation and currency risk

Key takeaways. Compare the two policy frameworks rather than headline rates alone; distinguish inflation composition and labour-market definitions; treat currency and financing outcomes as conditional.

Explanation / evidence

Use September for a matched headline-inflation comparison. October Swiss CPI is an additional update, not a matched comparison with Singapore. Singapore October inflation was not yet published at this cutoff. The latest observation and the latest comparable observation answer different questions.

Explanation / evidence

The equivalent Swiss report is the Monetary policy report within the SNB Quarterly Bulletin. Its Business cycle signals section adds company-level evidence. MAS presents its projections and supporting analysis in the Macroeconomic Review. Later monthly releases update each report's dated information set. [S02] [S22]

Explanation / evidence

Read the report in three passes. Start with the two policy systems, then compare inflation and employment, and finally examine CHF/SGD and financing risk. The source register and exact-value tables retain the evidence behind each conclusion.

02 / Policy frameworks

MAS manages an exchange-rate path; SNB sets a rate and can use FX intervention

In plain English: Read each instrument within its own policy framework.

MAS framework and SNB monetary-policy strategy; accessed 5 Nov 2026. [S06]-[S09] [S29]

Source assessment

Singapore uses its exchange rate because external prices reach the economy quickly. MAS describes a highly open economy in which imports account for almost 40 cents of domestic spending. The relevant policy variable is the trade-weighted S$NEER, not a peg to CHF or USD. Singapore interest rates are influenced by global rates and currency expectations. [S07]

Framework

Employment informs both policy assessments. Slack, vacancies, productivity and wages help indicate future price pressure. Neither framework should be relabelled as a simple rule to minimise the unemployment rate regardless of inflation. [S06] [S08] [S29]

03 / MAS decisions and projections

MAS tightened in April and July, then preserved that stance in October

In plain English: Read down the rows to see how the same-year forecast changed.

Central-bank forecasts, not realised inflation. All four decisions left the band centre and width unchanged. [S03]-[S05] [S01]

Source assessment

January was a normalisation assessment; April was an imported-cost response. January raised the inflation forecast but left policy unchanged. April anticipated wider pass-through from the energy disruption and increased the slope. July added a smaller increase as growth proved resilient and external costs remained persistent. [S03]-[S05]

Forecast / source assessment

The October hold is consistent with earlier tightening still working. The bank retained the appreciating path while narrowing its 2026 core forecast. Its 2027 core and headline forecasts were both 1.5-2.5%. An analyst should distinguish a smaller projected inflation outcome from a reversal in policy. [S01]

04 / Singapore monthly inflation

September inflation eased, but prices continued to rise

In plain English: Compare August with September within each component.

Annual inflation rates, %. September data published 23 Oct 2026. [S12] [S13]

Reported data

Disinflation means slower price increases, not necessarily falling prices. September headline inflation fell from 2.3% to 2.2% YoY, and core from 2.2% to 2.0%. Yet the monthly price changes were still +0.2% and +0.1%, respectively. A household can therefore experience rising prices while the annual inflation rate declines. [S13]

Analysis

Policy implication: assess breadth as well as direction. A single softer month is useful evidence, but it cannot establish that imported pressure has ended. Continued food and utility inflation can coexist with cheaper telecommunications and softer discretionary goods.

05 / Why Singapore inflation moved

Energy costs reach Singapore through both direct prices and business costs

In plain English: Follow both the direct price channel and the business-cost channel.

Conceptual transmission; no estimated weights or guaranteed timing. MAS April statement and September CPI report. [S04] [S12]

Source assessment

Timing explains why retail inflation can rise after the initial commodity shock. The September CPI report links the Q3 regulated electricity tariff to natural-gas prices observed mainly during April to mid-June. Contracts, inventories and tariff resets can delay pass-through. The exchange rate moderates the domestic-currency cost, but does not cancel the global supply shock. [S12]

Model estimate / source assessment

The Macroeconomic Review supports an external-shock lens. Box B estimates that external factors explain about 68% of inflation variation captured by its model. That is a model-based historical decomposition: it is neither an import weight nor a claim that 68% of today's inflation is imported. [S02], Box B

Analysis

The more important question is whether the shock spreads. If firms absorb cost increases in margins, the consumer impact is smaller. If demand is strong enough to sustain repeated price rises and workers seek compensating wage increases, the shock can become more persistent. Evidence on wages, productivity and services prices determines which mechanism is more plausible.

Source assessment

Policy works before the forecast horizon arrives. MAS cites a modelled peak effect after four to six quarters. That does not mean every import-price effect waits that long; it explains why policy should consider the medium-term path rather than react mechanically to the latest monthly number. [S08]

06 / Singapore employment

Employment growth accelerated while restructuring continued

In plain English: Keep net hiring, retrenchments and unemployment separate.

Employment changes and retrenchments are persons; unemployment is SA %. Q3 figures are advance estimates. MOM releases 15 Jun, 21 Sep and 30 Oct 2026. [S14]-[S16]

Reported data

Total job growth does not describe every worker's experience. Q3 gains were stronger for both residents and non-residents, but non-residents accounted for most growth, notably in construction and manufacturing. Resident gains were more concentrated in essential and public services. [S16]

Analysis

The inflation signal is mixed. Expanding labour supply can let output grow without an equivalent surge in wage pressure. At the same time, layoffs in outward-oriented industries point to restructuring and skill mismatch. A large net employment gain therefore cannot, by itself, establish a wage-price spiral.

Analysis

Watch matching as well as hiring. More job vacancies than unemployed people can coexist with unemployment if the available skills, location or pay do not match the positions. The relevant policy evidence is whether this mismatch produces persistent aggregate wage and cost pressure.

08 / SNB policy decisions

SNB kept the rate unchanged while the balance of risks evolved

In plain English: An unchanged policy rate does not imply unchanged conditions.

Official decisions; rates in % p.a. The June 2025 cut establishes the starting point. [S18]-[S21] [S48]

Source assessment

An unchanged rate can coexist with changing monetary conditions. Borrowers and exporters respond to market yields, credit terms and the exchange rate, not only the policy-rate announcement. The September discussion assessed that CHF depreciation outweighed modest interest-rate increases, easing overall conditions since June. [S23]

Analysis

The economic logic is conditional, not automatic. When underlying inflation is modest, raising interest rates solely to offset a temporary energy shock can unnecessarily weaken demand. Equally, holding rates becomes harder to justify if the shock raises broad wage and price expectations. The relevant issue is medium-term persistence.

Source assessment

FX intervention remains available. The removal of heightened intervention language later in the year did not abolish the instrument. The SNB still stated that it could act in the currency market when needed. Such willingness is different from evidence that a specific intervention actually occurred. [S18] [S23]

Survey intentions / forecast

Wage intentions add a domestic-cost check. Companies in the SNB's regional talks expected wage increases averaging 1.3% in 2027, following 1.5% in 2026. These are surveyed company intentions, not realised national wage growth. [S22], Business cycle signals

Timing

Do not backdate later information. The September decision used August CPI. The October inflation release arrived in November; it is new evidence for the next assessment, not an explanation the September policymakers could already have used.

Source assessment / later speech

A later speech reaffirms the transmission framework. On 5 November, Schlegel described the policy rate as the main instrument, with other measures when necessary. His discussion of rate increases, exchange rates and later inflation effects is a source assessment, not a new policy decision. [S52]

09 / Swiss monthly inflation

Swiss inflation accelerated after July, with imported prices leading

In plain English: Read inflation by origin; the series are not contributions.

CPI sub-index changes, % YoY. June-September bulletin and FSO October update. [S22] [S24] [S25]

Reported data

The monthly sequence contradicts a simple story of permanently negligible Swiss inflation. Headline inflation rose from 0.4% in July to 0.8% in August, 1.0% in September and 1.5% in October. The acceleration deserves monitoring even though the composition remains comparatively narrow. [S22] [S25] [S24]

Calculation / Reported data

Separate the change in the annual rate from the monthly price increase. October's YoY rate increased by 0.5 percentage points, from 1.0% to 1.5%. October's price level rose by 0.2% from September. These are different calculations; the former also reflects the year-earlier comparison base. [S24] [S25]

Analysis

A weaker franc can amplify an external cost shock. If an imported item costs more abroad and the franc weakens against the invoicing currency, both changes can lift the CHF price. This is a transmission mechanism, not an estimate of how much of October inflation was caused by exchange rates.

Analysis

The policy question is whether this spreads. Rising imported inflation paired with stable core inflation calls for a different assessment from an economy-wide acceleration in wages and service prices. The next section tests that distinction with the October composition.

10 / Swiss inflation composition

Swiss inflation remains concentrated in imports and energy

In plain English: Compare categories without adding overlapping baskets.

October CPI, % YoY unless stated. FSO release published 3 Nov 2026. [S24]

Analysis

The evidence favours a concentrated shock over general overheating. This is an interpretation of the distribution of price changes, not a claim that domestic inflation is zero. Domestic inflation and some service prices are positive; imported costs could still feed into them later.

Definitions

Core definitions change the comparison with Singapore. FSO core 1 excludes fresh and seasonal products, energy and fuel. MAS core excludes accommodation and private transport, but retains food and other energy exposure. A higher MAS core number is therefore not a clean measure of stronger wage-driven inflation. [S24] [S06]

Analysis

The next test is persistence. A retreat in energy inflation with stable core would support looking through part of the shock. Broadening services inflation, stronger wage settlements or rising expectations would make that interpretation less comfortable. The October data does not by itself settle the next SNB decision.

11 / SNB forecast revisions

SNB's 2026 inflation forecast rose despite the unchanged rate

In plain English: Read across for forecast years and down for revisions.

Annual-average CPI inflation forecasts, %. Conditional on a 0% policy rate over the forecast horizon; not a rate commitment. [S18]-[S21] [S29]

Calculation from forecasts

The forecast revision is informative even without a rate move. The 2026 forecast rose by 0.4 percentage points from the December 2025 vintage to September 2026: 0.7% minus 0.3%. Between June and September, the 2027 forecast rose by 0.2 percentage points. Those revisions indicate a changing outlook, not a mechanically fixed reaction function.

Explanation

Do not compare October's 1.5% directly with a 0.7% full-year forecast. A single monthly YoY inflation rate can exceed the calendar-year average. Testing whether a forecast is too low requires the full price-index path, the remaining months and its underlying assumptions. The headline comparison alone cannot prove a forecast error.

Conditional forecast

Conditional forecasts are not rate-path guidance. The SNB's constant-rate assumption differs from forecasts that embed an expected path of policy changes. Combining the two without adjustment would make a cross-country forecast comparison appear more precise than it is. [S29]

Analysis

September also improved the growth assessment. The SNB raised its 2026 GDP view to 1.5-2.0%, while retaining around 1.5% for 2027. Stronger activity reduced the case for immediate easing, even though underlying inflation remained modest. [S18]

12 / Swiss employment

The Swiss labour picture depends on the measure and seasonal adjustment

In plain English: Separate survey estimates from administrative registrations.

FSO Q1/Q2 survey and SECO October registered labour data. Percentage-point changes compare like definitions. [S22] [S26]-[S28]

Analysis

The apparent quarterly improvement disappears after seasonal adjustment. The unadjusted ILO rate fell between Q1 and Q2, while the adjusted rate edged higher. This is precisely why a monthly or quarterly policy narrative should keep seasonal treatment consistent rather than select whichever measure supports the preferred story.

Reported data / analysis

Employment and unemployment can rise together. The number working can increase while the labour force expands faster, or while some workers struggle to move into the new vacancies. The Q2 ILO unemployment rate was also above the 4.6% recorded a year earlier. The data therefore supports growth with residual labour-market slack, rather than a simple boom-or-recession label. [S27]

Definitions

Cross-country ranking would be misleading. Singapore's overall rate combines resident survey evidence with nonresident employment records. Switzerland's registered rate depends on administrative registration; its ILO survey is a separate measure. The direction within each well-defined series is more informative than comparing 2.0% with 3.0%. [S17] [S28]

13 / Growth and concentration

Strong export sectors can lift GDP without overheating every domestic market

In plain English: Compare like quarterly growth measures.

Real GDP; non-annualised changes. Singapore Q3 advance published 14 Oct; Swiss Q2 published 3 Sep 2026. [S31] [S43]

Reported data / analysis

Singapore's growth remains strong but has moderated on this vintage. Technology-related production supports the overall economy, while the latest advance estimate shows slower quarterly growth than Q2. The relevant inflation question is whether export demand spills into domestic wage costs, rents and consumption. [S43]

Reported data

Swiss Q2 strength has a large industry-specific component. SECO reports a 10.5% quarterly expansion in chemicals and pharmaceuticals, against 0.7% in the rest of manufacturing. Domestic final demand grew 0.5%. A large export-sector jump therefore should not be read as equivalent overheating in household demand. [S31]

Data vintage

Keep historical revisions out of causal claims. April's Singapore statement described Q1 using an advance estimate that was later revised. To understand why policy was set then, use the then-available data. To describe the economy now, use a consistent latest vintage. The two questions require separate tables.

17 / Why CHF/SGD moved

MAS can support SGD while low CHF rates encourage carry trades

In plain English: Treat each arrow as a conditional mechanism.

MAS October statement and SNB September discussion, published 22 Oct. No attribution percentages assigned. [S01] [S23] [S46]

Source assessment

The SNB documented stronger carry-trade incentives. Its September assessment described roughly 3% trade-weighted CHF depreciation since June. Wider foreign interest differentials and low currency volatility made CHF-funded carry trades more attractive, while safe-haven demand was limited. The October publication date should not extend that June-to-September measurement window. [S23] [S46]

Analysis

MAS supplies a separate channel. An appreciating trade-weighted policy band can restrain imported prices and support SGD, but MAS does not target CHF/SGD. CHF can still strengthen against SGD if Swiss-specific demand is strong enough. This distinction prevents a basket-policy statement becoming an unsupported bilateral forecast.

Identity

The cross-rate identity is exact. With both USD legs observed at the same time: CHF/SGD = (SGD per USD) ÷ (CHF per USD). Holding the other leg constant, a lower numerator or a higher denominator lowers the cross. If both move, their proportional changes determine the result. A chart of the cross alone cannot determine which leg supplied most of the move.

Analysis

Price-level and capital-flow arguments operate on different horizons. Lower Swiss inflation may matter for long-run purchasing power, yet low nominal rates and changing risk appetite can dominate shorter-term flows. Inflation differences alone do not generate a reliable near-term exchange-rate target.

18 / CHF financing implications

Low funding cost leaves currency and collateral risk intact

In plain English: Separate funding cost, currency translation and asset performance.

Conceptual balance-sheet comparison; no client balances, spreads or return assumptions supplied. SNB strategy and safe-haven discussion. [S29] [S30]

Contract distinction

The SNB rate is not a client loan quotation. A financing contract can include a reference rate, bank spread, tenor adjustments, fees and collateral conditions. Its reset date need not match a central-bank meeting. The 0% policy rate therefore cannot be entered as an all-in borrowing cost. [S29]

Identity

Track principal translation before adding other effects. For fixed principal, SGD liability = CHF principal × CHF/SGD. If interest increases the CHF amount owed, apply the exchange rate to that contractual amount. Do not subtract the same loan a second time from a portfolio NAV already reported net of financing.

Analysis

Carry is compensation for accepting risks, not a guaranteed spread. An unhedged borrowing strategy depends on the currency path as well as interest savings. Hedging replaces part of that uncertainty with a priced forward or option and may introduce rollover, basis, liquidity or premium costs.

Action

Practical action: assess the balance sheet in its spending currency. Record the CHF liability, interest reset date, asset currencies, hedged share and collateral headroom separately. This report supplies a research framework; it does not calculate personalised returns from missing loan or portfolio inputs.

20 / Conditional scenarios

The next move depends on which risk becomes persistent

In plain English: Watch the conditions; no probabilities or price targets are assigned.

Qualitative author analysis, as of 6 Nov 2026. Outcomes depend on persistence and market expectations; none is guaranteed.

Scenario analysis

The largest mistake would be treating one scenario as the forecast. A risk table identifies what would change the thesis. It does not claim that policymakers will react to any single data release in the way shown.

Analysis

Surprise matters alongside direction. A currency can weaken after a policy tightening if markets expected a larger one. Conversely, a hold can support a currency when investors expected easing. A credible trading interpretation needs the pre-decision market expectation, which this report does not estimate.

Risk framework

Use inversion to protect the decision. Instead of asking only why CHF might remain weak, ask what would make that view fail: a haven shock, broader Swiss inflation, a shift in SNB communication, or a weaker Singapore outlook. Those are the conditions that deserve explicit monitoring.

Historical context

History is a reminder about regime risk. The removal of the SNB's EUR/CHF minimum exchange rate in January 2015 illustrates that policy arrangements can change. It is not evidence that the same event will recur under today's different framework. [S47]
